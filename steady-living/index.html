Steady Living

A weekly hour of practical philosophy in Kigali.

Every week, a few of us sit down for one hour. Ten minutes of silence, one short passage from someone who thought hard about how to live, and an honest round on how the week actually went.

How do you stay steady when work, family and your own impulses all pull at you?

People have been working on that question for over two thousand years. We borrow from all of them: the Stoics, Epicurus, Buddhist thought, Aristotle. They disagree with each other in useful ways, and none of them get the last word. They are lenses, not a creed.

  • No religion
  • No performance
  • No reading required
  • One hour, same time every week

Why it exists

Kigali has plenty of church and cell-group communities, and a few yoga studios. It has almost nothing for people who want a reflective, secular practice: a place to slow down, look at the week honestly, and be heard without being advised or recruited. This circle sets out to fill that gap.

The hour

  • 5 min
    Arrive and settlePhones away. Quick hellos.
  • 10 min
    Silent sittingEyes closed or a soft gaze. Someone keeps time with a quiet timer.
  • 5 min
    One passage, read aloudShort enough to take in on a first hearing. Read twice, slowly.
  • 25 min
    The roundEach person has about four minutes on the week's question. No advice, no cross-talk. This is the heart of the hour.
  • 10 min
    Open questionOne question from the passage, talked through together.
  • 5 min
    Silence and closeA minute of quiet, then one line each: what I'll carry into the week.

A typical question for the round: Where did I hold the line this week, and where did I bend?

What we read

One school at a time, a month each, one short passage a week. The passage and the question go out the day before, so you can read them in thirty seconds if you want to. You don't have to.

  • StoicismWhat is and isn't in your control. Your judgment of a thing versus the thing itself. Getting up and doing the work.
  • EpicurusWhich desires are worth feeding. Pleasures that cost you later. Friendship as the thing that most makes a life good.
  • Buddhist thought, kept secularHow mood shapes what you see. The second arrow: pain is the first, your reaction adds the second. Watching an urge rise and pass.
  • AristotleWe become what we repeatedly do. Character as habit.

How we keep it honest

  1. No creed, no recruiting. Nobody has to believe anything. Religious people are welcome; nobody is converted to or from anything.
  2. A practice circle, not a debate club. We test ideas against our weeks, not against each other.
  3. What's said here stays here. Share the ideas freely, not other people's stories.
  4. No advice unless asked. Listening is the gift.
  5. Pass is always allowed. "I'll pass this week" is a complete answer.
  6. Start and end on time. The hour is protected. That's part of the practice.

It is

  • Small: three to six people
  • Fixed: same slot every week
  • Low-commitment: a four-week trial, then we decide together
  • Open to anyone, with or without a background in any of this

It isn't

  • A religious or spiritual group
  • A lecture or a reading seminar
  • Therapy, or a place to be fixed
  • Something you need to prepare for
